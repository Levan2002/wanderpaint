How to keep track of the US states you've visited

Keeping score of the US states you've visited sounds easy until you try to remember them all: that road trip through three states in a day, the layover you're not sure counts. Here's how to build a list you can trust, and keep adding to.

Decide what counts

The first question is the one nobody can answer for you: what counts as having visited a state? Some people say you have to have spent a night. Others say it counts if you stopped and did something there. A few count driving through. There's no official rule, so pick one and write it down. Whatever you choose, apply it to every state so your total stays honest.

Also decide how to treat Washington, D.C. and territories such as Puerto Rico or Guam. They aren't states, so they usually sit in a separate tally.

Start with what you can prove

Your memory will be patchy, and your photo library is better evidence. Anything taken with Location Services on records where it was shot. Go through old trips, weddings, conferences and road trips. You'll often find states you'd forgotten, and states you thought you'd visited but only flew over.

Write down dates, even rough ones

A list of names is fine, but a list with the year you first visited each state is better. It tells you which trips are worth revisiting, shows how your travelling has changed over time, and makes the list a record of your life rather than a checklist. Add a short note for the memorable ones.

Keep a wish list

Alongside the states you've visited, keep a separate list of those you want to go to. When you're planning a trip, a wish list makes it easy to see which nearby states you could add with a small detour.

Work through it by region

If you're trying to finish all 50, group the remaining states by region and plan trips that pick up several at once. Neighbouring states are often a day's drive apart, so a long weekend can add two or three.

A simple way to check yourself

Once you've got a list, test it. Pick five states at random and ask yourself what you did there, and when. If you can name a specific place or memory for each, your list is solid. If you can't, you may be counting a state you only crossed on a highway, which is fine if that's your rule, but it's worth knowing.

Common pitfalls

  • Counting flights. Flying over a state, or a photo taken from a plane window, isn't a visit by almost anybody's rule.
  • Counting airports inconsistently. If a layover counts for one state, it counts for all of them.
  • Losing the list. A note on your phone, a spreadsheet and a paper map will all drift apart. Keep one master list.
  • Forgetting the early years. Trips from childhood are easy to lose. Ask your family while you can.
